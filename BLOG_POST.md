# How a hardware wallet's "randomness" wasn't — and how we hunted the keys

*A plain-language account of the July 2026 COLDCARD Mk2/Mk3 entropy bug, why
two days of GPU scanning found nothing, and what we had to fix to look in the
right place.*

---

## The one-paragraph version

A COLDCARD is supposed to generate your Bitcoin seed from a true hardware
random number generator. For four years of firmware it didn't. Due to a silent
build mistake, the "random" seed actually came from a small, predictable
software generator. The only thing standing between an attacker and your money
thing standing between an attacker and a modeled clean-cold-boot wallet is a
single 32-bit `pad` plus the device's prior RNG-call history. If they know your
Bitcoin address — which is public — they can try candidates until one produces
your address, and then they have your key. Public incident lists show hundreds
of drained addresses; this research has **not yet proved** that the attacker
used this exact call history against any one of them.

## What "random" is supposed to mean

When you set up a hardware wallet it makes a 24-word seed phrase. Every key
your wallet will ever have is derived from that phrase. The whole point of the
device is that this seed is **un-guessable** — 256 bits of genuine randomness
from a dedicated hardware circuit (a TRNG).

If the seed is *not* truly random, everything built on it collapses. A seed
that came from a predictable source can be re-generated by anyone who figures
out the recipe.

## The bug, in three honest mistakes

No single person wrote a broken line. The failure lived **between** files,
which is why code review missed it for years.

1. **The board had good TRNG code — but marked "static".** The COLDCARD Mk3 has
   a real hardware RNG and code to read it. But that function was declared
   `static`, which makes it **invisible to the linker** outside its own file.

2. **A config flag picked the wrong `rng_get()`.** The build set
   `MICROPY_HW_ENABLE_RNG = 0`, telling MicroPython "this board has no hardware
   RNG." So MicroPython politely compiled in its **backup** `rng_get()` — a
   small software PRNG called *Yasmarang*, meant for chips that genuinely lack
   an RNG.

3. **A safety check checked the wrong thing.** The library that asks for
   random bytes (libngu) had a guard: `#ifndef MICROPY_HW_ENABLE_RNG → #error`.
   But `#ifndef` only checks the macro **exists**, not its **value**. The macro
   *was* defined — as zero. The guard passed, the build succeeded, and at link
   time the library bound to the **software** `rng_get()`, because that was the
   only globally-visible one.

Each file looks correct in isolation. The wrong wiring only exists at the
**linker boundary** — invisible to anyone reading source, and even to AI code
review (Coinkite confirmed their AI review missed it too).

## So how bad is it, really?

The fallback PRNG is seeded **once**, from:

```
pad = UID_low32 ^ SysTick     n = RTC->TR     d = RTC->SSR
```

- `UID_low32` is the 32-bit word read from the chip's fixed hardware unique ID;
  it is not necessarily the user-facing COLDCARD serial number.
- `SysTick` is a millisecond timer phase (~80,000 values).
- `RTC` — and here's the kicker — on the Mk3 the real-time clock is **never
  switched on** (`MICROPY_HW_ENABLE_RTC=0`). On a clean power-up its registers
  read **zero**.

So `n` and `d` are 0, and `UID ^ SysTick` collapses into **one 32-bit word**.
Not 2³² × 80,000 — just **2³²**, because the two values are XORed into a single
32-bit result. That is the *entire* device-specific part of this clean-boot
hypothesis: about 4 billion possibilities. The complete test is much slower
than a raw PRNG loop because every candidate must run BIP39 PBKDF2 and Bitcoin
key derivation. Our RTX 4090 measures about 290,000 source-faithful first-boot
candidates per second at one address index, completing one pad pass in roughly
four hours.

If that exact UID word is known from the affected device, the direction changes:
instead of testing all 4 billion pads, test only `UID32 XOR t` for the roughly
80,000 possible SysTick values. That is about **53,687 times fewer pad
candidates**. UID is therefore not required for a complete search, but it is
extremely useful for a cheap one. It still does not reveal the RTC state,
first-boot interaction history, address path, passphrase, or dice use.

**The address is the oracle.** Your Bitcoin address is public. An attacker
generates candidate seeds, derives the address each one would produce, and
stops when it matches yours. Whoever finds the match has your key — without
ever touching your device, and without moving any funds to prove it.

## Why we scanned for two days and found nothing

This is the part that matters, and the reason this writeup exists.

We built the whole pipeline and it **worked** — it recovered planted test keys
and even a third-party demo key, bit-for-bit, on CPU and GPU. So we pointed it
at the list of ~2,300 drained victim addresses and let it run. And run. **No
hits.**

The scanner wasn't broken. **We were searching the wrong place.**

The PRNG's output depends not just on its seed, but on **how many times it has
already been run** — every prior draw advances the state. Our early sweep only
tried the first few draws (`skip 0–8`), which corresponds to a wallet created
in a *later login session*.

But a **brand-new device, first wallet** — the classic setup — does a lot of
random-looking housekeeping *before* it ever makes your seed:

- it shuffles 32 empty storage slots,
- writes three 4 KB blocks of random noise (3,072 draws),
- shuffles again when you accept the Terms,
- and shuffles a little more every time you press a key.

That's **~3,100+ draws before your seed exists**. We were searching ~3,100
draws *too early*. The first-boot wallets literally were not in the space we
scanned. **Throwing more GPUs at it would have found nothing, faster.**

And it was subtler still: the firmware's random-number helper has a retry loop
that advances **one** of the two internal generators but not the other — by an
amount that depends on the candidate itself. So the two generators can't be
described by one shared "skip" number. They have to be simulated **independently,
per candidate**, following the exact boot sequence.

## The fix: model the boot, not a guess

We rewrote the search to replay the **actual first-boot sequence** from the
firmware source: shuffle → noise writes → keypad shuffles → Terms shuffle →
keypad shuffles → *then* draw the 32 seed bytes — simulating both generators
faithfully, per candidate.

We proved it three ways before spending a cent on the real search:

1. **Two independent CPU simulators** of the corrected flow produce
   **byte-identical** seeds across many inputs.
2. The **GPU's own debug output** matches those simulators exactly (raw bytes →
   entropy → address hash).
3. A **live positive control**: we planted a known first-boot key in a tiny
   search window and the GPU scanner recovered **exactly** it — pad, both skip
   values, and the private key.

Only then did we point it at the real target list.

## The result

The key-recovery machinery is proven, but the real-world claim remains open.

Before the latest budgeted run, source-hash-matched, positive-controlled GPU
shards had tested partial prefixes of Q=18,20,22,24,26,28,30 at address index
0. Together those negative ranges equal 3.43 complete 32-bit pad passes spread
across different setup histories. None matched the 2,311-address target corpus.

The current run resumes those exact checkpoints, completes Q=22 at index 0,
expands Q=22 to receive indexes 0–7, then uses the remaining budget to complete
the other standard PIN histories. **As of this writeup, no real-victim hit has
been recovered.** That is not evidence that the firmware bug is harmless; it
means only that the exhausted target/hash/history/index combinations were
empty.

If the budgeted plan also returns no hit, the next useful work is not another
identical pad pass. It is wider derivation coverage (change/account/BIP44/49),
contiguous keypad-session histories and alternate settings flows, empirical
traces from affected hardware, and independent classification of which public
addresses are actually attributable to affected Mk2/Mk3-generated seeds. The
complete decision tree and exact negative coverage are in
[`SEARCH_STATUS.md`](SEARCH_STATUS.md).

## What you should do

If your seed was made on a COLDCARD Mk2/Mk3 running firmware **4.0.1–4.1.9**
and you did **not** add at least 50 of your own private dice rolls:

1. Update to firmware **4.2.0 or later**.
2. Generate a **completely new** seed on the updated device.
3. Verify the new backup and a receive address.
4. Move your funds. (A firmware update alone does **not** repair an old seed.)

The same applies to Mk4/Mk5/Q on affected firmware, though those mix in extra
secure-element entropy and are less exposed. A strong, unique BIP39 passphrase
also changes the wallet and reduces risk.

## Why this matters beyond one bug

- **You can't verify randomness by looking at its output.** A deterministic
  generator produces beautifully random-looking numbers. Entropy is a property
  of the *source* — and only a build/link check proving *which* source is wired
  in can confirm it. Both of the firmware's sanity checks passed on the weak
  output, every time.
- **The dangerous bugs live at boundaries.** The crypto choice (moving to
  Bitcoin Core's library) was *correct*. The integration was not. Review must
  verify **end-to-end symbol resolution and call reachability**, not just that
  the right code exists somewhere in the binary. The fix does exactly that: it
  deletes the fallback and adds a build-time check so the wrong wiring fails
  the build instead of shipping.

## Scope & ethics

This research used **public data only** (drained, balance-zero addresses). No
funds were moved. Key control is demonstrated with **BIP322 message
signatures** — an inert proof that authorises nothing. Still-funded vulnerable
wallets must be **notified, never swept.**
